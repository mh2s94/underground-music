Hollow Tide turn rust into a religion on Rust Bloom

Nine tracks of slow collapse, recorded in one take with the amps left out in the rain. It is the most patient record the band has made, and the heaviest.

The first thing you notice is the room. Not the playing, not the riffs — the room, the low hum of a concrete box that has hosted one too many soundchecks and one too few renovations. Rust Bloom was recorded there over four days, and the record does not try to hide it.

A record that refuses to hurry

Where the previous release pushed forward at every turn, this one sits. Songs open with a single held note and wait for the listener to get uncomfortable. The payoff, when it arrives, is not louder so much as wider: three guitars tracked in the same room, all slightly out of tune with each other on purpose.

The band is not interested in being the loudest thing in the room. They are interested in being the last thing you hear.

That patience extends to the mix. Vocals sit low, drums sit dry, and the low end is allowed to smear across everything, the way it does when you are standing too close to the stacks.

What it costs

The trade is immediacy. Two of the nine tracks could lose ninety seconds each and not suffer, and the sequencing puts the weakest idea directly in the middle of the strongest run. It is a record that asks for the whole evening, not a commute.

Subtlety is still a strategy. It just happens to be a loud one.

Give it the evening. Rust Bloom is not a record that rewards skipping; the parts that feel like waiting are the parts that make the rest land.
